# Why we want an ecosystem

Status: Draft · Pass 0 · Updated 2026-06-07

> What this is: why GridWorks wants other companies to join, and what kind of
> businesses they should be. The vision is Jessica's, in her words; the grounding
> connects it to the GridWorks architecture and to *Redefining Demand Response*.

## The vision (Jessica's words)

> We want to rapidly expand infrastructures and — more importantly — functional
> ways of interacting that allow us to build communities, improve energy
> resilience, keep people warm. We want to collaboratively build out the map of
> the electric grid in the shared GNode Tree. We want to design sets of
> businesses that are fun to work for, really give the people in the
> organization the evident satisfaction of helping others, that pay a decent
> amount **but promise no huge upside**. What we are building is a mycelial
> network of humanity as it remembers how to engage in heart-centered ways.

## Grounding

**Infrastructure, but the interactions matter more.** The "more importantly"
is load-bearing. Copper and heat pumps are necessary, but the differentiator is
the *functional ways of interacting* — the shared language (ASL), the GNode
tree, the representation and dispatch contracts. An ecosystem is needed because
no single company can build the *interaction layer* alone; protocols only become
real when independent parties speak them. The reason to want companies joining
is the same reason a language wants speakers.

**The concrete ends: community, resilience, warmth.** These are not soft
add-ons; they are what the balancing resource *is for*. *Redefining Demand
Response* frames it precisely: renewable energy is abundant but variable, load
is currently oblivious, and the grid needs flexible load to become a
next-generation balancing resource — there are already places "ignoring
abundance" (negative prices behind transmission constraints). Keeping people
warm with thermal storage that leans into abundant hours is the same act as
stabilizing the grid. Resilience and warmth are two readings of one mechanism.

**The shared GNode Tree as a commons.** Most distribution utilities lack a
real-time (often even a static) picture of their low-voltage network, and even
when they have it, it is hard to share (`architecture.txt`). The GNodeAlias tree
is a way to build that map *collaboratively* — each participant contributing the
slice it can see. This is inherently a multi-party good: a map one company keeps
is a database; a map many companies build together is infrastructure.

**Businesses with no huge upside — by design.** This is the sharpest, most
deliberate constraint, and it is structural, not sentimental. "Decent pay, no
huge upside" is a choice *against* the venture-scale extraction pattern, in which
a network is captured and rents are pulled out of it. A mycelial network cannot
have a center that extracts; the moment one node is built to win big, it stops
sharing nutrients. Capping the upside keeps the incentives mutualistic — the
businesses stay oriented toward the evident satisfaction of helping others
rather than toward enclosure. (This is also why the architecture decentralizes
decision-making: each node decides locally while participating in global
harmony.)

**Mycelial, literally as an architecture.** Mycelium is distributed, has no
center, shares nutrients across the web, and is resilient precisely because no
single point owns it. That is the same shape as the GNode system — no central
controller, local decisions, global coordination through shared protocol. The
metaphor is not decoration; it is the system topology described in the language
of living things.
