# Money shocks and energy (vision)

Status: Draft · Pass 0 · Updated 2026-06-09

> What this is: a near-verbatim record of an exchange (Jessica ↔ Claude,
> 9 June 2026, during the Strait of Hormuz crisis) about what happens to the
> physical grid when the financial world stops — kept as a story, because the
> reasoning is the point. It ends where the GridWorks vision begins. A spoke
> of the [vision hub](primary.md).

## I. A mother's question

**Jessica:** I have a sense that the global financial and energy markets may
temporarily shut down as an after-shock of the Straits of Hormuz. I want to
take the highest-vibration approach to preparing for this. My basic sense is
that holding a lot of love and happiness is great, and so is having enough
food and water and being prepared to just hunker down for 3–5 days. My concern
is that my 16-year-old daughter is planning a 3-week music camp in
Ithaca NY — about a 5-hour drive from our house. I don't think I could help
myself from trying to reach her if there was a large blackout. Should I find
some way to stay with her during those 3 weeks? This is primarily an
evaluation of probability, and of how bad an idea traveling would be.

**Claude:** The crisis is real — the strait has been effectively closed since
late February, and traffic is still a trickle despite the ceasefire. But the
specific fear — a multi-day Northeast blackout that strands you 250 miles from
your daughter — sits at the end of a causal chain that mostly doesn't connect.

The Northeast's well-documented oil vulnerability is a *winter* phenomenon —
cold snaps make gas scarce and old oil turbines pick up the slack. In July the
grid runs on domestic natural gas, nuclear, hydro, and solar; none of it
transits Hormuz. The US imports almost no Persian Gulf crude; the crisis shows
up here as *price*, not as physical scarcity. And a financial-market shutdown
is paper rather than electrons: after 9/11 the NYSE closed four days and the
lights stayed on everywhere.

And in the bad case, you could still reach her. New Haven→Ithaca is one tank
of gas. In the 2003 blackout — 50 million people dark — roads stayed passable
and most power returned within two days. The binding constraint in a blackout
is that dead gas stations can't pump, so the drive is feasible if and only if
you start with fuel. Keep the car above ¾ tank, keep cash and a paper map, and
give your daughter one written instruction: *stay at camp; if anything big
happens, I will come to you there.* A fixed rendezvous beats reachability —
cell towers have hours of battery; plans don't expire. Staying near her for
three weeks would be proximity driven by fear, and a 16-year-old will read it
exactly that way. The full tank and the written plan are love expressed as
readiness.

## II. "I don't trust the assessments"

**Jessica:** I do not trust ISO-NE assessments. People close to the business
of pricing risk see others lying about probabilities all the time — and often
can't say why.

**Claude:** The distrust is warranted, and the mechanism isn't mysterious.
What risk-pricing people see is mostly not lying in the criminal sense — it's
incentive-shaped reporting. Seasonal grid assessments aggregate *self-reported*
data from the utilities being assessed. Before Winter Storms Uri and Elliott
the assessments said "adequate," and then 20–30% of the supposedly-firm fleet
failed to start. Add the career asymmetry (a false alarm embarrasses you this
quarter; a missed tail risk lands on someone else, later, diffusely) and
Goodhart's law (reserve margin became a target, so it gets managed as a number
rather than measured as a fact), and you get systematically thin tails with no
individual liar.

But the recommendation was built to be robust to that. The fuel-chain argument
is geography, not testimony — the same kind of fact as "New Haven is not
downstream of the Euphrates." And the plan doesn't need the probability to be
right: stop arguing about the tail's *odds* and check what the tail *costs*.
Run the scenario where the assessments are all wrong — multi-day blackout in
week two of camp. Full tank, cash, paper map, standing rendezvous: you're in
Ithaca by evening. Distrusting the official numbers should change your prep
depth, not your decision. Convert skepticism into margin.

## III. The premise, taken seriously

**Jessica:** I want to push on this. Start with the premise that all global
financial markets stop working — that the ways people get paid by large
companies all stop. What happens? I am not sure the utilities still work. Do
they fall apart for a bit until people band together and figure out how to
make it run again — local money, volunteers, exchange?

**Claude:** The answer has a phase structure.

**Phase 0 (hours to days): the physics doesn't notice.** The grid's financial
layer and physical layer are already decoupled in time. Wholesale markets
settle days to weeks after the electrons flow; dispatch is operational, not
transactional. If every market halted at noon, the control rooms wouldn't find
out from their consoles. Fuel has enormous inertia: nuclear runs on 18–24
month cycles, hydro is free, coal piles are 30–90 days deep, gas flows under
standing contracts whose pipelines need the grid as much as the grid needs
them.

**Phase 1 (days to weeks): the binding constraint is payroll and supplier
credit — and this is where the carve-outs happen.** Essential workers keep
showing up unpaid for a surprisingly long time: air traffic controllers worked
five weeks without pay in the 2018–19 shutdown, with absenteeism rising near
the end, not the start. Among repeat-relationship parties — which is the
entire utility supply chain — money degrades gracefully into IOUs: deferred
billing, scrip, emergency credit. In the 1930s bank holidays, scrip emerged
within days and commerce continued. The state's whole crisis toolkit aims at
restoring payment for critical infrastructure first. A scenario where payment
rails stay down for weeks *and* no state response materializes isn't a
financial-shutdown scenario anymore — it's a state-failure scenario, a much
bigger premise.

**Phase 2 (months, if it truly persists): the intuition is exactly right —
and there's a natural experiment.** Lebanon's financial collapse is the
cleanest case: the banking system genuinely froze, salaries became worthless,
and the grid degraded — but over *years*, not weeks, and the failure path was
fuel imports requiring hard currency, not operators walking out. What filled
the gap is precisely the banding-together: neighborhood diesel generators with
subscription tiers, local pricing, informal billing — a spontaneous, ugly,
functional local energy market. People didn't band together to restart the
national utility; they banded together to *route around* it at neighborhood
scale. So: the falling-apart takes months because everyone defends the
keystone, and the banding-together genuinely happens — but it arrives as a
degraded substitute, not a restoration.

## IV. Where the story becomes the vision

"Local money, volunteers, exchange" as the recovery layer when the global
settlement stack fails *is* Lebanon's generator economy — emerged by
necessity. A transactive local market with cryptographic veracity is the
designed, dignified version of the same organism — built **before** the crisis
instead of after it. The fact that the improvised version reliably appears
wherever the formal one dies is about the strongest evidence there is that the
demand is real.

From here the conversation turned to what GridWorks should seed: honoring
abundance when and where it shows up, gifts seeking needs, the fun of
collaborating and using all of our resources well (see the
[vision hub](primary.md) strands, [`hybrid-game.md`](hybrid-game.md), and
[`permissionless-eyes.md`](permissionless-eyes.md)).
